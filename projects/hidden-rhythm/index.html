The Hidden Rhythm of Partitions

How many ways can you write a number as a sum? The answers grow explosively, and yet in 1919 Ramanujan spotted a rhythm hiding in them that nobody had seen. This page lets you find it yourself.

Chapter 1

What is a partition?

A partition of n is a way to write n as a sum of positive whole numbers, where order does not matter. The number 4 has five of them: 4, 3+1, 2+2, 2+1+1 and 1+1+1+1.

Draw each part as a row of dots and you get a Ferrers diagram. Flip it across its diagonal and rows become columns: you get another partition of the same n, called the conjugate.

That flip is a bijection, the first counting trick in combinatorics: to count one family, count another one that is in perfect one-to-one correspondence with it.

Try it: pick a partition, press Conjugate, and watch every dot swap its row and column. Some partitions are their own conjugate.

Chapter 2

Three ways to count

The same question, three algorithms. Brute force writes down every partition and counts them. Dynamic programming fills a table of smaller subproblems, which is the generating function ∏ 1/(1−xk) in disguise. Euler's pentagonal recurrence gets each value from only about √n earlier ones.

Pick n and press Run. All three must land on the same number, and the work they do is very different.

Chapter 3

Ramanujan's rhythm

Colour every p(n) by its remainder after dividing by m, and lay the values out m to a row. For most m the wall looks like noise.

Move the slider to 5. One whole column goes dark: p(5k+4) is divisible by 5 every single time. Then 7 (column 5) and 11 (column 6) do the same. Ramanujan noticed this in 1919 while reading MacMahon's table of p(n).

p(5k+4) ≡ 0 (mod 5)   p(7k+5) ≡ 0 (mod 7)   p(11k+6) ≡ 0 (mod 11)

The pattern hunt below runs this scan live for every m from 2 to 36. Besides 5, 7 and 11 it flags 25 and 35. Neither is a new family: 25 is a stronger prime-power congruence (p(25k+24) ≡ 0 mod 25, due to Watson), and 35 follows from 5 and 7 by the Chinese remainder theorem.

What you see is a computer check on a finite range, not a proof. The congruences themselves are theorems; the proofs are in the report.

Chapter 4

How big does p(n) get?

Very big, very fast. p(100) has 9 digits, p(1000) has 32, p(10000) has 107. In 1918 Hardy and Ramanujan proved a formula that predicts the size:

p(n) ~ eπ√(2n/3) / (4n√3)

Drag n up to 10,000 and watch the ratio of the formula to the exact value settle toward 1. The exact values here are big integers computed with Euler's recurrence in your browser, no approximation.

In every case computed here the formula comes out a little too high. It is the leading form of the first term of Rademacher's exact series; keeping that whole term removes most of the error.
